Calculate the fixed payment required each period to pay off a loan
(or grow an investment to a target value) at a constant interest
rate — the formula behind every basic loan/mortgage payment
calculator.

## Return value

The fixed payment amount per period. Following Excel's cash-flow sign
convention, the result is **negative** when `pv` is positive (money
received now, like a loan), representing money paid out each period.

## Description

PMT assumes a loan or annuity with equal payments every period at a
constant interest rate — exactly the shape of a standard mortgage,
car loan, or fixed-payment amortization schedule. Two details cause
most of the confusion around this function: the sign convention, and
matching `rate`/`nper` to the same period length.

<Callout type="warn">
  Excel's financial functions treat money you **receive** as positive
  and money you **pay out** as negative. A $20,000 loan is a positive
  `pv` (you received it); the resulting `PMT` comes back negative
  (you're paying it out). This is correct, not a bug — wrap the whole
  formula in a unary minus (`=-PMT(...)`) if a positive payment number
  is what a spreadsheet actually needs to display.
</Callout>

## Common errors

### Payment amount is far too high or too low

<ExcelCode>{`PMT(5%, 30, 200000) → wildly wrong (5% used as a monthly rate on an annual term)`}</ExcelCode>

`rate` and `nper` must be in the **same period**. A 30-year mortgage
at 5% annual interest, paid monthly, needs the rate divided by 12 and
the term multiplied by 12:

<ExcelCode>{`PMT(5%/12, 30*12, 200000)`}</ExcelCode>

### The result is negative and that looks like an error

<ExcelCode>{`PMT(5%/12, 360, 200000) → -1073.64 (not a bug — see the sign convention above)`}</ExcelCode>

Not an error — `pv` was positive (money received), so the payment is
negative (money paid out). Negate the whole call if a positive number
is needed for display:

<ExcelCode>{`=-PMT(5%/12, 360, 200000)`}</ExcelCode>

### #NUM! — inconsistent or impossible inputs

A `rate` of `-100%` or below, or other combinations that make the
underlying annuity math undefined, return `#NUM!` — check the
inputs are realistic (a small positive rate, a positive `nper`).
