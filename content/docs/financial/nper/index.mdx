Calculate how many payment periods a loan or savings plan will take,
given a fixed payment amount and a constant interest rate — the
question "how long until this is paid off?" answered directly.

## Return value

The number of payment periods required, as a decimal number. A
result of `47.3` means the loan is paid off partway through the 48th
period — most amortization schedules round this up to a whole number
of payments.

## Description

NPER answers the same underlying loan-math question as PMT, PV, and
FV, just solved for a different unknown: instead of "what's the
payment?", it's "how many payments?" All four share the same
cash-flow sign convention.

<Callout type="warn">
  `pmt` and `pv` normally need **opposite signs** — a loan received
  (positive `pv`) is paid off with money going out (negative `pmt`).
  Passing both as positive numbers is the single most common cause of
  a `#NUM!` error or a nonsensical result from NPER.
</Callout>

## Common errors

### #NUM! — pv and pmt have the same sign

<ExcelCode>{`NPER(5%/12, 1000, 200000) → #NUM!`}</ExcelCode>

Both `pv` (200000) and `pmt` (1000) are positive here, which describes
an impossible cash flow — money coming in from two directions with
nothing going out. Make the payment negative to represent money paid
toward the loan:

<ExcelCode>{`NPER(5%/12, -1000, 200000) → 269.3`}</ExcelCode>

### The result looks far too large or too small

`rate` and `pmt` must be in the **same period**. A monthly payment
paired with an annual rate (instead of that rate divided by 12) will
produce a period count that's off by roughly a factor of 12.

### The answer isn't a whole number

NPER almost never returns a whole number — a fixed payment amount
rarely divides a loan evenly. Round up with
<ExcelCode>{`=ROUNDUP(NPER(...), 0)`}</ExcelCode>
to get the number of full payments actually required (the last one is
typically smaller than the rest).
