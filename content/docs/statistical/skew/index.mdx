Measure how asymmetric a dataset's distribution is around its mean —
positive means a longer tail stretching above the mean, negative
means a longer tail below it, and zero means a perfectly symmetric
spread.

## Return value

A skewness value: `0` for a perfectly symmetric distribution,
positive for a right-skewed one (a longer tail above the mean),
negative for a left-skewed one (a longer tail below it).

## Description

A skewed distribution has most of its values clustered on one side,
with a longer "tail" stretching out on the other — household income
is a classic real-world example of right skew, since most people
earn a moderate amount while a small number earn vastly more,
pulling the tail (and the mean itself) upward. SKEW quantifies that
asymmetry as a single number.

<Callout type="warn">
  SKEW treats the data as a **sample** representing a larger
  population, and needs at least 3 values to compute (its formula
  divides by `(n−1)(n−2)`). For the population version, use `SKEW.P`
  instead.
</Callout>

With the last value at `5`, the dataset is nearly symmetric and SKEW
comes out essentially to `0`. Stretching that value out to `9` (mild
right skew) or `14` (strong right skew) pulls SKEW progressively
higher, quantifying exactly what "a long tail on the high side" looks
like numerically.

## Common errors

### #DIV/0! — fewer than 3 data points

<ExcelCode>{`SKEW(5, 8) → #DIV/0!`}</ExcelCode>

SKEW's formula divides by `(n−1)(n−2)`, which is zero when there are
only 1 or 2 values — at least 3 are required.

### Misreading the sign of the result

A **positive** SKEW means the tail stretches toward higher values
(often with a mean pulled above the median), not that the data is
"more skewed toward small numbers" — the sign describes the
direction of the tail, not which side has more of the data packed
together.
