confined to a fixed interval (by default 0 to 1), commonly used to
model a probability, proportion, or conversion rate itself as an
uncertain quantity.

## Return value

With `cumulative` `TRUE`: the probability that a beta-distributed
random variable is less than or equal to `x`. With `cumulative`
`FALSE`: the density of that curve at `x`.

## Description

The beta distribution is uniquely suited to modeling quantities that
are themselves confined to a bounded range — most naturally a
probability or proportion between 0 and 1. Its two shape parameters,
`alpha` and `beta`, together determine everything about the curve's
form: equal values produce a symmetric curve, `alpha > beta` skews it
toward 1, and `alpha < beta` skews it toward 0. It's the standard
choice in Bayesian analysis for representing uncertainty about an
unknown probability — like a website's true conversion rate, before
or after observing data.

<Callout type="idea">
  With `alpha = 1` and `beta = 1`, the beta distribution reduces
  exactly to the uniform distribution on `[A, B]` — every value in
  the interval becomes equally likely, with no skew in either
  direction.
</Callout>

shape is toward the lower end of the range.

## Common errors

### #NUM! — x is outside the [A, B] interval, or alpha/beta isn't positive

<ExcelCode>{`BETA.DIST(1.5, 2, 5, TRUE) → #NUM!, since 1.5 is outside the default [0,1] range`}</ExcelCode>

`x` must fall between `A` and `B` (0 and 1 by default), and both
`alpha` and `beta` must be strictly positive.

### Forgetting A and B rescale the entire interval, not just x

<ExcelCode>{`BETA.DIST(50, 2, 5, TRUE, 0, 100) → treats x on a 0-100 scale, not the default 0-1`}</ExcelCode>

When `A` and `B` are supplied, `x` is interpreted on that rescaled
interval throughout — `alpha` and `beta` still control the same
underlying shape, just stretched to fit the new range.
