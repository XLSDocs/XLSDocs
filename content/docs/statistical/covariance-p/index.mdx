Measure how two datasets vary together — positive means they tend to
rise and fall together, negative means one tends to fall as the
other rises — treating the data as the complete population being
measured.

## Return value

The population covariance between `array1` and `array2` — the
average of the product of each pair's deviations from their own
means.

## Description

Covariance is the raw building block `CORREL` is normalized from —
positive covariance means the two datasets tend to move in the same
direction, negative means they tend to move in opposite directions,
and the magnitude depends on the scale of the original data (unlike
CORREL, which is always between -1 and 1 regardless of scale).

<Callout type="warn">
  Covariance's magnitude **can't be compared across different
  datasets** the way CORREL's can — a covariance of `1000` might
  represent a very strong relationship for small numbers or a weak
  one for large numbers, since it's not normalized. Use `CORREL` when
  the goal is judging or comparing the *strength* of a relationship,
  not just its calculation.
</Callout>

## Common errors

### Comparing covariance magnitudes across different units

<ExcelCode>{`COVARIANCE.P in dollars vs. COVARIANCE.P in thousands of dollars → wildly different magnitudes for the same underlying relationship`}</ExcelCode>

Covariance scales with the units of the original data — converting
one dataset from dollars to thousands of dollars changes the
covariance by a factor of 1000, even though the actual relationship
hasn't changed at all. CORREL is scale-independent and doesn't have
this problem.

### Using COVARIANCE.P when the data is actually a sample

If `array1`/`array2` represent a sample meant to estimate a larger
population, `COVARIANCE.S` (dividing by `n − 1` instead of `n`) is
the statistically appropriate choice instead.
