Return the right-tailed probability of the chi-square distribution —
the area under the curve to the right of a given value, matching the
p-value `CHISQ.TEST` reports internally.

## Return value

The probability that a chi-square-distributed random variable with
`deg_freedom` degrees of freedom is greater than or equal to `x` —
the area under the curve to the right of `x`.

## Description

CHISQ.DIST.RT is the mirror image of `CHISQ.DIST`'s cumulative
result: `CHISQ.DIST.RT(x, df)` always equals
`1 - CHISQ.DIST(x, df, TRUE)`. Because a large chi-square statistic
means the observed data diverges sharply from what was expected, the
right tail — "how likely is a statistic at least this extreme" — is
the direction that actually matters for a goodness-of-fit test, which
is exactly what `CHISQ.TEST` returns.

<Callout type="idea">
  Given raw observed and expected counts, `CHISQ.TEST(actual_range,
  expected_range)` and `CHISQ.DIST.RT(SUMPRODUCT((actual_range -
  expected_range)^2 / expected_range), degrees_of_freedom)` return the
  same result — CHISQ.TEST just computes the statistic and looks it
  up in one step instead of two.
</Callout>

result on the identical input.

## Common errors

### #NUM! — x is negative, or deg_freedom is less than 1

<ExcelCode>{`CHISQ.DIST.RT(2, 0) → #NUM!`}</ExcelCode>

`x` must be non-negative and `deg_freedom` must be at least 1.

### Confusing this with CHISQ.DIST's left tail

<ExcelCode>{`CHISQ.DIST.RT(9.488, 4) → 0.0500, not the same as CHISQ.DIST(9.488, 4, TRUE) → 0.9500`}</ExcelCode>

These are complementary, not interchangeable — a small
CHISQ.DIST.RT result is evidence of a real difference (the usual
goal of a goodness-of-fit test), while a small CHISQ.DIST result
would mean the observed statistic is unusually *close* to the
expected pattern.
