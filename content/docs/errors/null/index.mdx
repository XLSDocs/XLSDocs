`#NULL!` is the rarest of Excel's classic error types, and almost
always comes from one specific mistake: a space typed between two
ranges where a comma was meant, which Excel reads not as a typo but
as its own real operator — the **intersection operator** — asking for
the cells the two ranges have in common.

## What actually breaks

<ExcelCode>{`=SUM(A1:A5 C1:C5)`}</ExcelCode>

A comma between two ranges means "both of these." A space between two
ranges means something entirely different: "only the cells common to
both." `A1:A5` and `C1:C5` share no cells at all — different
columns — so their intersection is empty, and `SUM` has nothing to
add. The formula isn't malformed; it's asking a real, well-formed
question that happens to have no answer.

<Callout type="info">
  The intersection operator is genuinely useful when the two ranges
  **do** overlap — `=SUM(A1:C1 A1:A10)` returns the single cell `A1`,
  the one cell common to a row and a column. `#NULL!` specifically
  means this operator was used (intentionally or not) on two ranges
  that share nothing.
</Callout>

## Common causes

**A missing comma between function arguments** — by far the most
common cause. A single accidental space where a comma belongs turns
"sum these two ranges" into "sum what they have in common":

<ExcelCode>{`=SUM(A1:A5, C1:C5)  → correct: adds both ranges
=SUM(A1:A5 C1:C5)   → #NULL! (space instead of comma)`}</ExcelCode>

**Referencing two genuinely non-overlapping named ranges with a
space**, intending to combine them rather than intersect them —
same underlying mistake, harder to spot since named ranges don't show
their cell addresses directly in the formula.

## How to fix it

1. **Check every space between range references** in the formula —
   if a comma was intended, that's the fix.
2. **If the intersection was actually intended**, confirm the two
   ranges really do share the cells you expect — a typo in either
   range's bounds (wrong row or column) is enough to make an
   intended intersection come up empty too.

## Catching it in a formula

[IFERROR](/docs/logical/iferror) suppresses `#NULL!` along with every
other error type. [ERROR.TYPE](/docs/info/error-type) returns `1`
for it specifically — the first code in its table, reflecting how
rarely it comes up in practice compared to the other six classic
errors.

## Not the same as #REF! or #VALUE!

`#REF!` means a reference that existed is now gone. `#VALUE!` means
an input's type is wrong. `#NULL!` is narrower and stranger than
both: every reference in the formula is valid and correctly typed,
but a specific *operator* — intersection — was asked to do something
that has no answer for the ranges given.
