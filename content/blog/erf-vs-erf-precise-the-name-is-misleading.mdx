`ERF.PRECISE` reads like an upgrade — a more accurate version of
`ERF`, the way `ATAN2` is a more careful version of a plain arctangent.
That's not what's going on here. For the same input, the two functions
return a bit-for-bit identical number.

## What "PRECISE" actually means in this pair

<Callout type="idea">
  `ERF.PRECISE(x)` and `ERF(x)` — the one-argument form — compute the
  exact same value. The "PRECISE" suffix isn't describing extra
  accuracy; it's just matching a naming pattern Excel 2010 introduced
  alongside a handful of other functions (`ERFC`/`ERFC.PRECISE` being
  the direct sibling pair).
</Callout>

<ExcelCode>{`=ERF(1)           → 0.8427
=ERF.PRECISE(1)   → 0.8427`}</ExcelCode>

If there's no accuracy difference, what's actually different? `ERF`
has a **second, optional argument** `ERF.PRECISE` doesn't: an
`upper_limit`. Plain `ERF(x)` integrates from `0` to `x`; `ERF(a, b)`
integrates between any two bounds directly, in one call.

<ExcelCode>{`=ERF(0.5, 1)       → 0.3222   ' integrates from 0.5 to 1 directly
=ERF(1) - ERF(0.5) → 0.3222   ' the same answer, computed manually`}</ExcelCode>

That's the entire distinction. `ERF.PRECISE` is `ERF` with that
optional second argument removed — a narrower function, not a more
accurate one.

## The sibling pair makes it even less intuitive

`ERFC` and `ERFC.PRECISE` — the complementary versions, each equal to
`1 - ERF(x)` — don't even have that much of a difference between them.
Neither one ever had a two-argument form to begin with, so there's
nothing `ERFC` can do that `ERFC.PRECISE` can't, or the other way
around. They're fully interchangeable, full stop.

<ExcelCode>{`=ERFC(1)          → 0.1573
=ERFC.PRECISE(1)  → 0.1573`}</ExcelCode>

Unlike `ERF`/`ERF.PRECISE`, where at least one of the pair has a real
extra capability, `ERFC`/`ERFC.PRECISE` is a naming-consistency
addition with literally nothing functional attached to it.
