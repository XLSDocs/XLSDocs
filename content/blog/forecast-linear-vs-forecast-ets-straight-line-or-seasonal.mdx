Both functions predict a future value from historical data, and both
ship with modern Excel. The difference isn't in how accurate either
one is in the abstract — it's whether your data has a repeating
up-and-down shape that a single straight line simply can't represent.

## The same history, two different shapes of answer

<ExcelCode>{`=FORECAST.LINEAR(6, {10,20,30,40,50}, {1,2,3,4,5}) → 60`}</ExcelCode>

Five months of sales climbing by exactly 10 each time.
`FORECAST.LINEAR` fits one straight line through those points and
extends it — month 6 comes out to 60, month 7 to 70, and so on. There's
no seasonality to model here, so a straight line is the whole story.

<ExcelCode>{`=FORECAST.ETS(DATE(2026,4,1), quarterlySales, quarterlyDates) → 165`}</ExcelCode>

Now take two years of quarterly sales that peak every Q2 and dip every
Q3/Q4, riding on top of a steady upward trend. `FORECAST.ETS` picks up
on **both** patterns at once — the Q2 2026 forecast (165) is higher
than every historical Q2, continuing the trend, while still landing
above the Q3/Q4 forecasts the same way every prior year did. A
straight-line fit through that same data would average the peaks and
dips away entirely, missing the shape a reader would spot on a chart
in two seconds.

## The test that actually decides it

<Callout type="idea">
  Plot the data. If it just goes up (or down) in roughly a straight
  line, `FORECAST.LINEAR` (or plain `FORECAST` — they're identical)
  is the right tool and the simpler one. If it has a repeating shape —
  a holiday spike, a weekday/weekend swing, a Q2-is-always-the-peak
  pattern — `FORECAST.ETS` is built specifically to keep projecting
  that shape forward, not just the overall direction.
</Callout>

`FORECAST.LINEAR` genuinely can't see seasonality even if you want it
to — it only ever fits one line. `FORECAST.ETS` can still handle
trend-only data with no seasonal shape at all (pass `seasonality = 1`
to skip pattern detection), so it's the more general tool, at the
cost of needing more historical data to work with — auto-detecting a
seasonal pattern requires at least two full cycles of history, while
a straight line can technically be fit through as few as two points.

## Why FORECAST.LINEAR exists at all

`FORECAST.LINEAR` calculates the exact same result as the original
`FORECAST` function — there's no difference between them. It was
added in Excel 2016 purely so the straight-line method would have an
equally explicit name alongside the new `FORECAST.ETS`, not because
`FORECAST` needed fixing. If compatibility with older Excel files
matters, plain `FORECAST` is the safer choice; if not, either works
identically.

## What FORECAST.ETS actually needs to work

Two things `FORECAST.LINEAR` doesn't care about at all: `timeline`
values have to be **evenly spaced** (daily, monthly, quarterly — any
regular interval), and there has to be enough history to see a
suspected pattern repeat **at least twice**. A monthly seasonal
pattern needs 24+ months of data before `FORECAST.ETS` will trust
it's real rather than coincidence; with less, it falls back to a
trend-only forecast automatically.
