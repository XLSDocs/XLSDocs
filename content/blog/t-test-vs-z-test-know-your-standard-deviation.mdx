Both functions test whether a sample's results are likely real or
just chance. Both return a probability, not a raw score. And it's
easy to reach for whichever one you used last time, since they answer
what sounds like the same question. The actual deciding factor is
narrower than either name suggests.

## The question neither function's name answers

<Callout type="idea">
  Do you genuinely **know** the population's standard deviation in
  advance — from a specification, a long-established process, or
  some other outside source — or are you estimating it from the
  sample itself? If it's truly known, `Z.TEST`. If you're estimating
  it (which is almost always the real situation), `T.TEST`.
</Callout>

That's the whole distinction. Sample size, number of groups, one-tail
or two — none of that decides which function to use. Only whether the
standard deviation came from somewhere outside the sample, or from
the sample.

## The same kind of question, two different tools

<ExcelCode>{`=Z.TEST(scores, 85, 5) → 0.0100`}</ExcelCode>

Five test scores (mean 90.2), tested against a hypothesized mean of
85, with a **known** population standard deviation of 5 supplied
directly. Z.TEST returns 0.0100 — strong evidence the true mean is
really above 85.

<ExcelCode>{`=T.TEST(group1, group2, 2, 2) → 0.0761`}</ExcelCode>

Two independent groups of 5 values each, compared with T.TEST instead
— no population standard deviation supplied anywhere, because T.TEST
never takes one. It estimates the spread from the samples themselves,
which is exactly why it's the far more commonly correct choice: in
real analysis, the true population standard deviation is essentially
never known outright.

## Why the wrong choice matters

Z.TEST assumes the standard deviation it's given is exactly right. If
that number is actually just an estimate dressed up as a known value,
Z.TEST's result understates how much uncertainty is really in play —
it doesn't know the estimate itself carries error. T.TEST's
underlying t-distribution has deliberately fatter tails than the
normal distribution Z.TEST uses, which is precisely the extra margin
that accounts for not knowing the standard deviation for certain. That
gap matters most with small samples and shrinks as sample size grows,
but it never fully disappears.

## T.TEST also asks a second question Z.TEST doesn't have

Z.TEST always compares one sample against a hypothesized mean. T.TEST
covers three different comparison shapes through its `type` argument
— paired (matched before/after measurements on the same subjects),
two-sample assuming equal variance, and two-sample assuming unequal
variance (Welch's test, the safer default when in doubt). Picking the
wrong `type` on the same data can noticeably change the answer:

<ExcelCode>{`=T.TEST(group1, group2, 2, 1) → 0.1916   ' paired
=T.TEST(group1, group2, 2, 2) → 0.0761   ' equal variance
=T.TEST(group1, group2, 2, 3) → 0.0781   ' unequal variance`}</ExcelCode>

On the identical two groups, treating unrelated data as paired
(`type = 1`) gives a meaningfully different — and wrong — answer here,
since these two groups were never actually matched pairs.
