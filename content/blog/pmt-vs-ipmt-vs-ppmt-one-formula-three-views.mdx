Three functions, nearly identical argument lists, and a genuinely
common mix-up: using PMT when a payment's principal/interest split
is actually what's needed, or reaching for IPMT/PPMT when the total
payment amount was the only question. They're not competing
functions — they're three views of the exact same loan.

## The same loan, sliced three ways

<ExcelCode>{`=PMT(rate, nper, pv)
=IPMT(rate, per, nper, pv)
=PPMT(rate, per, nper, pv)`}</ExcelCode>

PMT returns the **total** fixed payment for the whole loan — the
same number every period. IPMT and PPMT split one *specific*
payment (identified by `per`) into the portion that's interest and
the portion that actually pays down the balance. Add IPMT and PPMT
together for any given `per`, with identical `rate`/`nper`/`pv`, and
the result always equals PMT exactly — they're not three independent
calculations, just three ways of describing the same cash flow.

<Callout type="warn">
  `IPMT(rate, per, nper, pv) + PPMT(rate, per, nper, pv)` always
  equals `PMT(rate, nper, pv)`, for any valid `per`. If a hand-built
  amortization schedule doesn't satisfy this, the bug is in that
  schedule, not in the functions — an easy sanity check that's worth
  building into any spreadsheet that computes both.
</Callout>

## Why IPMT and PPMT need an extra argument

PMT doesn't take a period number because it doesn't need one — the
payment amount is constant for the life of a fixed-rate loan. IPMT
and PPMT both take `per` because their answer genuinely changes every
period: interest is charged on whatever balance remains, and that
balance shrinks with every payment. `per` tells the function which
specific payment in the loan's life to break down.

## The split shifts dramatically over the loan's life

On a $200,000, 5%, 30-year loan, the fixed payment is $1,073.64 every
single month. But how much of that is interest changes enormously:

| Payment | Interest | Principal |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $833.33 | $240.31 |
| 180 (halfway) | $567.81 | $505.84 |
| 360 (last) | $4.45 | $1,069.19 |

The first payment is 78% interest. The last is 99.6% principal. Both
add up to exactly $1,073.64 — PMT never changes, but the IPMT/PPMT
split moves almost the entire distance from one extreme to the other
over the life of the loan.

## A rule of thumb

Reach for PMT alone when the only question is "what's the payment" —
budgeting, a quick loan comparison, a mortgage calculator's headline
number. Bring in IPMT and PPMT the moment the split itself matters:
building a full amortization schedule, figuring out how much of this
year's payments are tax-deductible mortgage interest, or explaining
to someone why their loan balance barely moved after two years of
"on-time" payments.
