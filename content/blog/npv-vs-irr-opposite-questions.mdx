Both functions take the same shape of input — an initial investment
and a series of uneven future returns — and both are standard tools
for deciding whether a project is worth doing. The difference is
which piece of the math each one solves for, and it trips people up
in a very specific way once the two get mixed together.

## Same cash flows, different unknown

NPV takes a discount rate you already know (a cost of capital, a
required return) and tells you what the cash flows are worth in
today's dollars. IRR takes the same cash flows and works backward: it
finds the exact discount rate that would make their net present value
equal to zero.

<ExcelCode>{`=NPV(rate, 15000, 18000, 20000) - 50000
=IRR({-50000, 15000, 18000, 20000})`}</ExcelCode>

Feed IRR's result back into NPV as the rate, on the same cash flows,
and NPV comes back to (essentially) zero — that's not a coincidence,
it's the actual definition of what IRR is solving for. On the
`-50000, 15000, 18000, 20000` example above, IRR returns about
**2.83%**; plug 2.83% into NPV against the same three return values
and the result lands within a few dollars of the $50,000 investment
it's being compared against.

## The trap: they don't agree on where the investment goes

<Callout type="warn">
  NPV expects the **initial investment left out** of the function
  call, subtracted separately afterward:
  `NPV(rate, futureCashFlows...) - investment`. IRR expects the
  **exact opposite** — the investment goes *inside* the array, as its
  first (usually negative) entry: `IRR({-investment, cashFlow1, ...})`.
  Applying NPV's convention to IRR (or vice versa) silently produces a
  wrong number, not an error.
</Callout>

This isn't a minor footnote — it's the single most common way both
functions get misused, precisely because they look like they should
work the same way. NPV assumes every value you hand it happens one
full period in the future, so an investment made *today* (time zero)
has to stay outside the call or it gets incorrectly discounted. IRR,
on the other hand, needs the investment as part of the same array it's
finding a break-even rate for — there's no "outside the call" option.

## A worked comparison

Same $50,000 investment, three different sets of return years, one
number to compare them by:

- Conservative returns (12,000 / 14,000 / 16,000): IRR ≈ **-7.89%**
- Base case (15,000 / 18,000 / 20,000): IRR ≈ **2.83%**
- Aggressive returns (18,000 / 22,000 / 26,000): IRR ≈ **14.30%**

That's IRR's actual job: turning three different uneven cash-flow
schedules into one comparable number, without needing to already know
a discount rate to plug into NPV first.

## When to reach for which

Use NPV when the discount rate is already settled — a company's
known cost of capital, or a required return that's a policy decision,
not something the analysis is trying to discover. Use IRR when the
question is closer to "what return does this actually generate,"
especially when comparing several differently-sized opportunities
that don't share one obvious discount rate to test them all against.
