A waterfall chart answers a different question than a bar chart. A
bar chart says "here are five numbers." A waterfall chart says "here's
how we got from this number to that number" — starting cash, plus
revenue, minus costs, equals ending cash, each step shown as a bar
that floats from where the last one left off.

## The fast path: Excel's native Waterfall chart

Since Excel 2016, **Insert → Charts → Waterfall** builds one of these
automatically from a plain list of values. Right-click any bar you
want treated as a running total (not a delta) and choose **Set as
Total** — that's what tells Excel to anchor it at zero instead of
floating it from the previous bar. For most day-to-day use, this is
genuinely the right tool: no formulas, no stacked-column trick.

The manual build below is worth knowing anyway — for versions before
2016, for tools that don't have a native waterfall type, and because
building it by hand is what makes the "why does the ending bar not
match" failure mode (see below) obvious instead of mysterious.

## Step 1: get the running total right

Every floating bar's position depends on the running total *before*
it, not just its own value. The classic locked-range approach:

<ExcelCode>{`=SUM($B$2:B2)`}</ExcelCode>

Copied down, this gives a running total after each row. The bar for
any given row then needs to float between **yesterday's** running
total and **today's**:

<ExcelCode>{`running_before = running_total one row up
running_after  = running_total this row`}</ExcelCode>

## Step 2: separate total bars from delta bars

A total bar (Starting Cash, Ending Cash) and a delta bar (Revenue,
COGS) are drawn completely differently — one anchors at zero, one
floats — so the data needs a column marking which is which:

<ExcelCode>{`=IF(C2="total", 0, MIN(running_before, running_after))`}</ExcelCode>

This is the "base" value for a stacked-column chart: for a total bar
it's `0` (the bar starts from the bottom of the chart), for a delta
bar it's the *lower* of the two running-total checkpoints (the bar
starts from wherever the lower edge actually is, whether that's
rising or falling).

## Step 3: build it as a stacked column (the manual technique)

Without the native chart type, a waterfall is a stacked column chart
with three series layered on top of each other:

- **Base** — the formula above, given **no fill** so it's invisible.
  This is what pushes each visible bar up to float at the right
  height instead of starting from zero.
- **Increase** — `=IF(AND(C2="delta", B2>0), B2, 0)`, filled green.
- **Decrease** — `=IF(AND(C2="delta", B2<0), [ABS](/docs/math/abs)(B2), 0)`,
  filled red.

Total bars need a fourth series the same way, filled a distinct
color, so Starting Cash and Ending Cash read differently from the
deltas between them.

## The part that trips people up

The ending total bar isn't calculated from the chart — it should be
an **independently known, verified number**: the actual ending cash
balance from your accounting system, not a formula that just sums the
deltas above it. That distinction is the entire point of putting it
on the chart at all.

If you instead compute Ending Cash as `=SUM(deltas) + Starting Cash`,
the chart can never disagree with itself — it'll always "balance,"
even if a real expense category is missing from the list entirely.
Pull the ending number from an independent source, and a waterfall
chart becomes a genuine check: if the bars' running math doesn't land
on the verified ending number, something real is unaccounted for,
and the chart just told you before anyone had to go looking for it
by hand.
