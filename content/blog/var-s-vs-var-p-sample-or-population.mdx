Type the same five numbers into `VAR.S` and `VAR.P`, and you'll get
two different answers — not because one of them is wrong, but because
they're built to answer two genuinely different questions about the
same data, and nothing about typing the numbers in tells Excel which
question you meant to ask.

## The same data, two different results

<ExcelCode>{`Values: 40, 55, 48, 62, 45
=VAR.S(values) → 74.5
=VAR.P(values) → 59.6`}</ExcelCode>

Both functions compute the same underlying idea — how spread out the
values are from their average — but they divide by a different
number to get there. `VAR.S` divides by `n − 1` (4, here); `VAR.P`
divides by the full count `n` (5). That single divisor is the entire
difference, and it's never trivial: `VAR.S` is always the larger of
the two on identical data.

## The actual question, not the formula

<Callout type="warn">
  The choice isn't about which formula "looks more correct" — it's
  about what the five values in your sheet actually represent. Are
  they **everything** you're measuring (VAR.P), or are they a
  **sample** standing in for a larger group you didn't fully measure
  (VAR.S)?
</Callout>

Five students' test scores, when those five students are the entire
class: use `VAR.P` — there's no larger population being estimated,
these numbers are the whole story. Five students' scores, pulled as a
sample to estimate how an entire school district performs: use
`VAR.S` — the extra division by `n − 1` corrects for the fact that a
small sample tends to understate the true spread of whatever larger
group it's drawn from.

## Why the correction exists at all

A sample's own variance, if you just divided by `n` like the
population formula does, is a slightly biased (too-small) estimate of
the real population variance it's meant to represent — smaller
samples are more likely to miss the true extremes sitting further out
in the full population. Dividing by `n − 1` instead of `n` inflates
the result just enough to correct for that bias on average. It's not
an arbitrary tweak; it's the mathematically derived fix for a real,
provable underestimation.

## It gets more confusing before it gets simpler

Excel actually ships **four** variance functions, not two — `VARA`
and `VARPA` apply the identical sample/population distinction, but
also count text as `0` and `TRUE`/`FALSE` as `1`/`0` instead of
ignoring them the way `VAR.S`/`VAR.P` do. Unless a column genuinely
mixes real numbers with logical values on purpose, `VAR.S` and
`VAR.P` are almost always the right pair to reach for — the "A"
versions solve a narrower, less common problem.
