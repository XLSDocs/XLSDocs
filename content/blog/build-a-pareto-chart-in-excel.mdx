A support team logs 202 tickets in a month across nine root causes.
Fixing all nine would take a quarter. Fixing the right four might fix
most of the problem by next week — but only if you can tell which
four those are. That's the question a Pareto chart answers: sort the
causes by how much damage they do, then show how quickly their combined
share adds up.

## Step 1: sort the causes, largest first

The chart only works if the bars are sorted descending by count — that's
what makes the "how fast does this add up" shape visible at all. If
your data already lives in a table, the fastest way is Excel's dynamic
[SORT](/docs/arrays/sort) function rather than a manual Data → Sort
that needs re-running every time the numbers change:

<ExcelCode>{`=SORT(A2:B10, 2, -1)`}</ExcelCode>

`2` sorts by the second column (the count), `-1` means descending.
Keep "Other" out of this step — it's a catch-all bucket, not a cause
with its own count to compete on, and it belongs at the end of the
chart regardless of where its number would otherwise land (see the
last callout above).

## Step 2: compute the cumulative percentage

This is the column that actually builds the line. The classic formula
locks the denominator with `$` and copies down:

<ExcelCode>{`=SUM($B$2:B2)/SUM($B$2:$B$10)`}</ExcelCode>

Copied down, `B2` in the numerator's range grows one row at a time
while the denominator stays fixed — a running total divided by the
grand total. It works, but it depends on nobody breaking the `$` lock
when the row range changes later.

<ExcelCode>{`=SCAN(0, B2:B10, LAMBDA(acc,v, acc+v)) / SUM(B2:B10)`}</ExcelCode>

[SCAN](/docs/custom-functions/scan) spills the whole running-total
column in one formula, with no reference to lock and nothing to
accidentally break on a copy-down. Divide the whole spilled array by
the grand total and the cumulative-percent column is one formula
instead of nine copies of one.

<Callout type="info">
  Excel also has a built-in Pareto chart: select your data, then
  **Insert → Charts → Insert Statistic Chart → Histogram → Pareto**.
  It sorts and adds the cumulative line automatically. Worth using when
  you just need the chart — building it manually, as above, is what
  you want when the 80% cutoff and the tie-break have to be visible
  and explainable, not just drawn.
</Callout>

## Step 3: mark the 80% line, and read it correctly

The dashed line at 80% isn't decoration — it's the actual cutoff for
"the vital few." Whichever bar's cumulative percentage first reaches
or passes 80% marks the last cause worth treating as a priority; every
cause after it is the "trivial many," individually small enough that
fixing them one at a time isn't worth the effort relative to the first
few.

In the chart above, that's **Broken CSV export** at 82.2% — meaning
the top 4 of 9 causes (Login failures, Slow page loads, Billing
errors, Broken CSV export) account for the large majority of ticket
volume, and the other 5 causes combined don't reach the remaining 20%
on their own merits.

## Step 4: handle ties honestly

Missing dark mode and Notification bug are tied at 8 tickets each.
[RANK.EQ](/docs/statistical/rank-eq) handles this the way most people
expect but don't always get from a formula: tied values share the
better rank, and the next distinct value skips the position that would
have gone to the tie:

<ExcelCode>{`=RANK.EQ(B6, $B$2:$B$10)`}</ExcelCode>

Both tied rows return rank 6. The next row down — Sync delay at 3
tickets — returns rank 8, not 7. If two causes are genuinely tied,
the chart should show that, not quietly break the tie by row order,
which is what a plain sort does if you don't check for it.

## The part that trips people up

The single most common mistake isn't in the formulas — it's assuming
the chart is finished once the bars are sorted and the line is drawn.
The real output of a Pareto chart is the sentence it lets you say out
loud: "fixing these N causes addresses 80% of the volume." If nothing
in your data update process re-sorts the bars and recomputes the
cumulative line when new tickets come in, the chart quietly goes stale
and starts pointing at the wrong "vital few" — which is exactly why
steps 1 and 2 above use SORT and SCAN instead of a one-time manual
sort and a fragile locked-range formula.
